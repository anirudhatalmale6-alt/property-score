Choosing where

Module 03 Written in full

Almost everyone picks the location backwards. They look for the place with the highest nightly rate, because that is the number Airbnb shows you. The nightly rate is not the business. The gap is the business.

The only ratio that matters

You pay rent twelve times a year whatever happens. You earn on the nights you fill. So the question is not "what can I charge?" but "how many nights must I fill before I have earned a single pound, and can I realistically fill more than that?"

Work it out like this, for any flat you are looking at:

StepExample
Rent the landlord wants, per year£1,100 × 12 = £13,200
Bills, wifi, council tax, insurance, per year£3,360
Fixed cost you must cover£16,560
What one booked night leaves you, after the platform fee and the cleaner£152
Nights you must fill before you earn anything109
Nights a well-run listing there actually fills~223
Your headroom114 nights

That flat works. A flat where you must fill 190 nights to break even and the market gives you 210 is not a business, it is a job with a twelve-month liability attached. The scorer does this arithmetic for you — switch it to "I rent it and sublet it" and it shows the break-even line and the headroom directly.

The London trap

London looks like the obvious place: highest rates in the country. It is the hardest place in the UK to do this. A whole home in Greater London can only be let short-term for 90 nights a year without planning permission for change of use, and Airbnb enforces the limit on its own platform. Run the sum above with 90 nights instead of 223 and almost every London flat fails.

People do operate in London. They do it with planning consent already in place, or by letting rooms rather than whole units, or by targeting stays over 90 days where the cap does not apply, which is a corporate-let business with different customers. None of those are the beginner version, and anyone selling you the beginner version in London is not telling you about the cap.

What a good location has

  • Demand that is not only in August. A city with hospitals, universities, courts, an airport or a big employer has midweek demand all year. A seaside town has eight brilliant weeks and a long silence, and the rent does not pause for the silence.
  • Rent that has not caught up. You want somewhere the long-let market is cheap relative to what visitors pay. That is usually a street or two out from the obvious postcode.
  • More than one reason to visit. A town that empties when one factory closes or one festival moves is a town where your whole year depends on somebody else's decision.
  • Walkable to the reason people come. Ten minutes on foot from the station, the hospital or the front is worth more than a bigger flat twenty minutes out.
  • No local restriction closing in. Check the council's website for licensing schemes and Article 4 directions before you fall in love with a postcode.

Where the rules differ across the UK

WhereWhat binds you
London90 nights a year for a whole home without planning permission. This is the single biggest constraint in the country.
ScotlandA short-term let licence is mandatory everywhere — operating without one is an offence. In control areas such as Edinburgh you also need planning permission, which is often refused.
WalesTo be taxed as a business rather than council tax you must be available 252 days and actually let 182. Miss it and some counties charge a council tax premium of up to 300%.
England, outside LondonA registration scheme is legislated but not yet fully live, and a separate planning use class has been consulted on. Treat today's freedom as temporary.
Northern IrelandTourism NI must certify the property before you can advertise it at all.

How to check a place without guessing

  1. Open Airbnb, search the town, set dates for a random Tuesday in November. Not August — anywhere looks busy in August. What is still available tells you what the quiet half of the year is.
  2. Note the nightly rates of six flats of the size you want. Ignore the outlier asking £400.
  3. Open the listings' calendars. Blocked dates months out are bookings. A calendar that is wide open in ten weeks' time is telling you something.
  4. Open Rightmove for the same postcode and find what that flat rents for long term.
  5. Do the break-even sum above. If the headroom is under about 40 nights, walk.
One honest warning about the numbers

Airbnb shows you asking prices, not achieved prices. A listing displaying £150 a night that sits empty is not a £150 listing. This is why the scorer gives you a range rather than a single confident figure, and why the range gets wider when your comparables disagree with each other. Anyone who hands you one number for a market has either bought expensive data or is guessing.

Finding landlords who will say yes

Module 04 Written in full

This is the part people find hardest, and the part the courses are vaguest about. It is not a secret technique. It is understanding what a landlord actually wants, then being the person who offers it, in writing, without wasting their time.

What a landlord is actually buying

They are not buying "passive income". They already have that. What keeps landlords awake is the gap: the void months, the tenant who stops paying, the Section 21 that now takes the best part of a year, the call about the boiler at ten on a Sunday night, and the state of the place at the end.

So what you are selling is certainty and condition. Rent on the first of the month, every month, including the months it is empty, because the emptiness is your problem not theirs. No tenant to evict, because there is no tenant. A property cleaned professionally between every stay rather than once every two years. And it comes back furnished and in better condition than it went out.

You are not offering them more money. Usually you are offering slightly less than the open market rent, and that is fine, because you are removing the risk they are paid to carry. Landlords who understand this say yes quickly. Landlords who want both the top rent and no risk are not your customer, and you should find that out in the first ten minutes rather than the fourth meeting.

Where they actually are

1. Letting agents — the fastest route, and the most misunderstood

Agents are not the enemy and they are not the decision-maker. They are a gate. What an agent wants is a placed property and a fee with no comeback on them. Ask them directly for a company let: a tenancy in your limited company's name, with written permission for short-term letting. Say those words. An agent who has heard the phrase before will tell you within a minute whether their landlords do it.

Ask specifically about properties that have been on their books more than a month, properties that need work, and landlords who have just had a bad tenancy. Those are the ones with a motivated owner.

The mistake that ends most of these deals later

An agent saying "yes that's fine" is not consent. Most agents have no authority to permit subletting, and their word evaporates the moment a neighbour complains or the property changes hands. You need it from the landlord, in writing, naming short lets specifically. Module 6 is entirely about this because it is the thing that turns a working business into a lost deposit and a claim for your profits.

2. Landlords advertising directly

OpenRent, Gumtree, SpareRoom and local Facebook groups are full of people letting their own property without an agent. They are the decision-maker, so you can have a real conversation immediately. They are also less likely to have heard of this arrangement, so you will do more explaining.

3. Landlord associations and local meetings

The National Residential Landlords Association has regional branches and meetings. Local landlord groups meet in pubs and function rooms in most sizeable towns. Go, say plainly what you do, and be the person who understood the rules. You will meet people who own six flats and are tired of four of them.

4. Property that has been empty a while

A flat that has been listed for six weeks is costing its owner money every day. Filter by longest listed. That is the whole technique.

5. Landlords who are quietly leaving the market

Between the tax changes, the interest rate rises and tightening regulation, a lot of small landlords want out but do not want to sell into a slow market. A guaranteed rent arrangement gives them an exit from the work without an exit from the asset. This is currently the warmest group in the country.

Two approaches to avoid

Letters through doors of empty houses. Slow, and it mostly reaches people who are not landlords and are now annoyed.

Answering a normal tenancy advert without saying what you plan to do. This is how people end up in breach from day one. Say what you are doing in the first message, every time. You will get fewer replies and every one of them will be worth having.

What to say

Short, specific, and it names the thing. If they are going to say no, you want the no today.

First message to a private landlord

Hello — I saw your flat on Nelson Road.

I run short-stay accommodation locally for contractors and visiting families. I'm looking to take a property on a three-year company let: I'd pay you £1,050 a month, on the 1st, twelve months a year including any month it sits empty, and I'd cover the bills, the cleaning and the maintenance under £150.

In exchange I'd need your written permission to let it on a short-stay basis. I'd furnish it at my own cost and it comes back to you furnished at the end.

I know that's not the arrangement you advertised, so if it's not for you that's completely fine. If it is worth a conversation I can come and see it this week.

Note what that message does. It names the rent, so nobody wastes an afternoon. It names the permission, so the deal cannot quietly fail later. It gives them an easy way to say no, which makes the yes real. And it does not use the phrase "rent to rent", which a lot of landlords have now read bad things about — not because you are hiding anything, but because "company let with written consent for short stays" describes it more precisely.

The objections, and honest answers

They sayYou say
"My mortgage won't allow it.""Worth checking — some buy-to-let products do permit it with consent, and some don't. If yours doesn't, that's a genuine no and I'd rather we found out now."
"What if you stop paying?""Then you'd remove me the same way you'd remove any tenant, and you'd still have the deposit and the furniture. I'd also suggest a break clause you can use at six months if it isn't working."
"Strangers in my property every week?""Guests are ID-verified and reviewed, there's a minimum stay, and the place is professionally cleaned between every booking. In practice it's inspected more often than a tenanted flat."
"I could get £1,200 on the open market.""You could, in the months it's let. I'm offering £1,050 for all twelve, with no voids, no arrears and no eviction. Whether that trade is worth £150 a month is genuinely your call."
"It's leasehold.""Then let's read the lease before either of us spends more time on it. If the head lease bars short lets, your freeholder can act against you even if you're happy, so it isn't a risk worth taking."
The number you should expect

Most people approach five landlords, get five noes, and conclude the model doesn't work. The realistic figure is dozens of conversations for one signed agreement, and the first one is the slowest because you have no track record to point at. Everyone selling a course showing three properties in ninety days is showing you the survivor. Budget for a few months of asking, and make the asking cheap — short messages, said plainly, to a lot of people.

Why the tone is like this

There is a great deal of material online promising three properties in ninety days and £10,000 a month. Most of it skips the consent question entirely, which is the one thing that decides whether somebody keeps their money. A course that tells people the difficult parts is slower to sell and much harder to complain about, and it is the only kind worth putting your name on.

This is education, not advice. Nothing here is legal, tax, planning or financial advice. Rules differ by nation and by council and they are changing quickly. Check the lease, check the mortgage conditions, check your council's own website, and get the consent in writing.
